The Life & Works of José Rizal

Experiences, Ideas, Choices & Actions

Discover how José Rizal's personal experiences, education, beliefs, and decisions shaped his dedication to justice, reform, and the future of the Philippines.

Understanding the National Hero

The Making of a Filipino Patriot

Rizal's life demonstrates how personal experiences, intellectual development, principled decisions, and meaningful actions can contribute to social change.

01 — Experiences

Experiences That Shaped His Beliefs

José Rizal's childhood and education played an important role in shaping his understanding of society and colonial injustice. Born in Calamba, Laguna, on June 19, 1861, he grew up in a family that valued education, discipline, literature, and learning. His mother, Teodora Alonso, served as his first teacher and introduced him to reading and literature at an early age.

One of the most significant experiences of his childhood was his mother's imprisonment in 1871. Teodora was accused of participating in an alleged attempt to poison her brother's wife. She was arrested, forced to travel from Calamba to the provincial jail in Santa Cruz, Laguna, and detained for approximately two years, with some accounts reporting two and a half years, before her eventual acquittal. This ordeal exposed Rizal to the consequences of an unfair colonial justice system and likely contributed to his growing awareness of abuses of authority.

Rizal continued his studies in Manila, attending the Ateneo Municipal de Manila and the University of Santo Tomas. In 1882, he traveled to Spain to pursue further studies. His time in Europe, including his stays in Spain, France, and Germany, exposed him to new intellectual traditions, scientific developments, and discussions about liberty, equality, citizenship, and government. These experiences broadened his perspective and helped him examine the conditions of Filipinos under Spanish colonial rule.

Through education, travel, and observation, Rizal developed a deeper understanding of the social and political challenges facing his country. His experiences encouraged him to use his knowledge and talents to advocate for a more just and enlightened society.

02 — Ideas

Ideas About Education, Equality, and Reform

Rizal believed that education was essential to the improvement of individuals and society. He wanted Filipinos to have opportunities to acquire knowledge, develop critical thinking, understand their rights, and participate meaningfully in their country's future. He demonstrated this belief during his exile in Dapitan, where he established a school and taught young students.

He also challenged the discrimination and prejudices directed at Filipinos during Spanish colonial rule. Through his essays and novels, Rizal questioned the abuses of certain colonial officials and religious authorities, the unequal treatment of Filipinos, and the obstacles that prevented people from receiving justice.

Rizal supported peaceful reforms, greater representation, and equal treatment under the law. As a member of the Propaganda Movement, he helped bring attention to Philippine conditions and advocated reforms through writing, public discussion, and intellectual exchange. He believed that people could work toward social progress by becoming informed, questioning injustice, and cooperating for the common good.

His ideas were expressed in essays such as The Indolence of the Filipino and The Philippines a Century Hence. Rather than accepting negative stereotypes about Filipinos, he examined the historical and social circumstances that contributed to the country's difficulties and considered the possible consequences of continued colonial oppression.

03 — Choices

Decisions Guided by His Principles

Rizal's decisions reflected his commitment to peaceful reform, civic cooperation, and national improvement. On July 3, 1892, he founded La Liga Filipina, an organization intended to unite Filipinos and promote mutual assistance, economic development, education, and peaceful social reform. Its founding demonstrated his belief that organized cooperation could help address the problems facing Philippine society.

Only days after the organization was established, Rizal was arrested and subsequently exiled to Dapitan. Despite his separation from Manila's political and intellectual circles, he continued serving others through medical practice, teaching, scientific interests, and community projects. His years in exile demonstrated that his commitment to the improvement of society extended beyond political writing.

Rizal did not join the armed revolution that began in 1896. He disagreed with the uprising's timing and approach, believing that the country needed further preparation and that education and peaceful reform were essential to lasting progress. However, his position should not be reduced to a simple rejection of Philippine independence: his writings increasingly questioned colonial rule and explored the country's political future.

His choices reveal a person who sought to act according to his convictions, even when those convictions brought him into conflict with colonial authorities and revolutionary contemporaries. They also demonstrate the complexity of his political beliefs during a period of profound change.

04 — Actions

Turning Ideas Into Meaningful Action

Rizal transformed his observations and beliefs into action primarily through literature, journalism, education, and public service. His novel Noli Me Tangere (1887) exposed abuses, social inequality, and the suffering experienced by Filipinos under colonial rule. Its sequel, El Filibusterismo (1891), presented a darker examination of injustice, oppression, and the consequences of a society resistant to meaningful reform.

Rizal also contributed essays to La Solidaridad, a newspaper associated with the Propaganda Movement. In The Indolence of the Filipino, he challenged the claim that Filipinos were inherently lazy by examining the historical and economic conditions that discouraged productivity. In The Philippines a Century Hence, he considered the country's political future and warned about the consequences of continued injustice and the refusal to introduce reforms.

During his exile in Dapitan from 1892 to 1896, Rizal continued to put his beliefs into practice. He treated patients, taught students, conducted scientific investigations, and participated in projects that benefited the local community. These activities reflected his conviction that knowledge should serve practical purposes and improve people's lives.

Rizal was arrested, tried by a Spanish military court, and executed on December 30, 1896, at Bagumbayan in Manila. His execution made him an enduring symbol of courage, sacrifice, and the struggle for justice. His final poem, Mi Último Adiós (My Last Farewell), expressed his profound attachment to his homeland and became one of the most significant works associated with his legacy.

Through his writings, educational efforts, and service to others, Rizal demonstrated how ideas could be transformed into actions that influenced public consciousness and helped shape the development of Filipino nationalism.
