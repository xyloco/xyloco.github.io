José Rizal: Life & Works

The Works of Dr. José Rizal

Novels, essays, poetry, plays, letters, historical research, translations, and creative work that challenged colonial injustice and helped shape Filipino national consciousness.

A writer beyond the novels

Words as a means of awakening

Dr. José Rizal (1861–1896) was a novelist, poet, essayist, historian, translator, and visual artist. He wrote primarily in Spanish, while also producing work in Tagalog and other languages. His writing exposed abuses under Spanish colonial rule, argued for education and civic dignity, and encouraged Filipinos to understand their history and identity.

His two best-known novels, Noli Me Tángere and El Filibusterismo, are central to his legacy, but they are only part of a much wider body of work. Rizal also wrote political and cultural essays, poems, plays, letters, and annotations to an early Spanish history of the Philippines. He translated literature, collected folklore, and made paintings, sketches, and sculptures. The National Commission for Culture and the Arts discusses Rizal within Philippine literature in Spanish.

Fiction

The two major novels

Rizal used fiction to make the realities of colonial society visible to readers. The novels are linked, but their tones and conclusions differ.

Berlin, 1887 · First novel

Noli Me Tángere — “Touch Me Not”

The novel follows Crisóstomo Ibarra as he returns to the Philippines after studying in Europe and tries to improve his community. Through Ibarra, María Clara, Elias, Sisa, and others, Rizal portrays clerical abuse, racial prejudice, corruption, unequal justice, and the suffering of ordinary Filipinos. The title comes from a Latin expression meaning “Touch me not.”

Why it matters: Rather than simply attacking individuals, the novel reveals social structures that allow injustice to continue. It helped readers discuss conditions that colonial authorities often preferred to keep unchallenged.

Ghent, 1891 · Second novel

El Filibusterismo — “The Reign of Greed”

The darker sequel returns to a society still shaped by oppression. Ibarra reappears as Simoun, a wealthy jeweler who seeks to bring down the colonial order through a plot for violent upheaval. The novel explores revenge, despair, political manipulation, education, and the moral costs of pursuing freedom through destructive means.

Why it matters: The novel deepens the questions raised by Noli Me Tángere and asks what happens when reform is denied and anger turns toward revenge.

Unfinished novel · Written in the 1890s

Makamisa

Makamisa is the title commonly associated with a fragment of an unfinished novel discovered among Rizal’s papers. Because the manuscript is incomplete, it should not be treated as a finished third novel in the same sense as Noli Me Tángere and El Filibusterismo. Its surviving pages nevertheless offer a glimpse of Rizal’s continuing literary plans.

Public argument and reform

Essays and political writings

Rizal’s essays addressed patriotism, colonial policy, education, social prejudice, history, and the future of the Philippines. Many appeared in reformist publications, including La Solidaridad.

1882 · Essay

El Amor Patrio / “Love of Country”

Often identified as Rizal’s first published essay, it presents patriotism as a serious duty grounded in concern for one’s homeland. It was published in Diariong Tagalog under the pen name Laong Laan.

Work overview and context
1889–1890 · Four-part essay

Filipinas Dentro de Cien Años / “The Philippines a Century Hence”

Rizal examines the country’s past under Spanish rule and considers possible futures. He warns that a government that refuses meaningful reforms risks losing its hold on the colony. The essay is a historical and political argument about colonial power and national destiny.

Work overview and context
1890 · Five-part essay

Sobre la Indolencia de los Filipinos / “The Indolence of the Filipinos”

Rizal challenges the colonial stereotype that Filipinos were naturally lazy. He argues that social and economic conditions—including abusive governance, disrupted trade, and lack of opportunity—helped produce the conditions described as indolence. He treats the issue as a historical and political problem rather than an inherent national trait.

1889 · Letter / public statement

Sa Mga Kababaihang Taga-Malolos / “To the Young Women of Malolos”

Written after young women in Malolos, Bulacan, sought permission to establish a school where they could study Spanish, the letter praises their determination and emphasizes education, independent judgment, dignity, and the role of women in society.

Work overview and context
1884 · Speech

Toast to Juan Luna and Félix Resurrección Hidalgo

Delivered in Madrid to honor Filipino artists Juan Luna and Félix Resurrección Hidalgo after their success in an art exhibition. Rizal used the occasion to celebrate Filipino talent and argue for equality and shared human dignity beyond race and nationality.

Other essays and articles

Writings for La Solidaridad

Rizal contributed articles and commentary to the reform movement’s newspaper. His public writing advocated reforms, representation, civil rights, education, and an end to abuses. The works belong to a wider conversation among Filipino reformists in Europe and the Philippines.

Verse and feeling

Poems

Rizal’s poems reflect youthful ambition, love of country, friendship, memory, exile, and his final farewell. Titles may appear in Spanish, Tagalog, or English translation.

1879 · Prize-winning poem

A la Juventud Filipina / “To the Filipino Youth”

Written for a literary contest when Rizal was a young student, the poem calls on Filipino youth to develop their talents and use knowledge and creativity for the good of the homeland. It is frequently remembered for its faith in the potential of the younger generation.

1886 · Germany

A las Flores de Heidelberg / “To the Flowers of Heidelberg”

Written while Rizal was in Heidelberg, the poem addresses flowers as messengers that might carry his affection and longing back to the Philippines. It connects the beauty of a place abroad with homesickness and national feeling.

1895 · Dapitan

Mi Retiro / “My Retreat”

This reflective poem describes Rizal’s life in exile at Dapitan, including the natural surroundings and the quiet he found there, while also expressing the distance between himself and his homeland.

Dapitan period

Himno a Talisay / “Hymn to Talisay”

Written for the boys who studied with Rizal in Talisay, Dapitan, the hymn celebrates learning, useful work, discipline, and a life shaped by community and nature.

1896 · Final poem

Mi Último Adiós / “My Last Farewell”

Written shortly before Rizal’s execution on December 30, 1896, this farewell poem expresses love for the Philippines, acceptance of sacrifice, and hope that the country may one day be free. The manuscript was left with his family. The poem became one of the most enduring texts associated with his life and death.

Other well-known poems

Selected additional poetry

Other poems associated with Rizal include Mi Primera Inspiración (“My First Inspiration”), Canto del Viajero (“Song of the Wanderer”), Un Recuerdo a Mi Pueblo (“Memories of My Town”), A Josefina (“To Josephine”), Kundiman, Me Piden Versos (“They Ask Me for Verses”), Por la Educación Recibe Lustre la Patria (“Through Education the Motherland Receives Light”), and Adiós a Leonor (“Farewell to Leonor”). Titles and translations vary across editions.

Drama and performance

Plays and dramatic works

1880 · Literary contest

El Consejo de los Dioses / “The Council of the Gods”

An allegorical play written for a contest honoring Miguel de Cervantes. Its classical subject gave Rizal a way to display his literary learning and skill in dramatic composition.

1880 · Ateneo performance

Junto al Pasig / “Beside the Pasig”

A short dramatic work written for performance at the Ateneo Municipal de Manila. It combines religious and symbolic elements and shows Rizal’s involvement in student theatre as well as poetry and prose.

Personal writing and public occasions

Letters, speeches, and correspondence

Letters to family

Rizal’s correspondence with his parents, siblings, and other relatives records his studies, travels, financial concerns, health, hopes, and experiences abroad and in exile. These letters also help historians reconstruct the circumstances in which he wrote and worked.

Letters to fellow reformists

Rizal corresponded with Filipino intellectuals and reformists, discussing political ideas, publications, personal matters, and the movement for reform. The correspondence is an important source for understanding the relationships behind the Propaganda Movement.

Speeches and commemorative addresses

Rizal’s speeches—including the toast honoring Luna and Hidalgo—show how he used public occasions to defend Filipino ability and dignity. These texts should be read in their historical context and not separated from the reformist debates of the period.

Recovering the past

Historical research, annotation, and folklore

Paris, 1890 · Historical edition

Annotations to Antonio de Morga’s Sucesos de las Islas Filipinas

Rizal prepared an annotated edition of Antonio de Morga’s 1609 account of the Philippines. His notes challenged colonial assumptions and highlighted evidence of Filipino society, culture, trade, and political life before Spanish rule. The project was part of his effort to encourage Filipinos to study their history critically.

Historical background and further reading · Philippine eLib catalogue

“The Monkey and the Turtle”

Rizal documented and illustrated a version of the Philippine folktale commonly known as “The Monkey and the Turtle.” Its publication helped bring Philippine folklore to an international scholarly audience and reflects his interest in local storytelling and cultural heritage.

Folklore and illustration overview

Ethnographic and linguistic interests

Rizal collected words, stories, and observations about Philippine life and languages. His interests in language and culture supported his wider effort to document Filipino identity rather than allow it to be defined only by colonial accounts.

Literature across languages

Translations and adaptations

Friedrich Schiller’s William Tell

Rizal translated Schiller’s drama into Tagalog. The story of resistance to tyranny resonated with his interest in liberty and civic courage, while the translation helped make European literature accessible to Filipino readers in their own language.

Translation overview

Hans Christian Andersen’s tales

Rizal translated selected tales associated with Andersen into Tagalog. These efforts show his interest in bringing literature to new readers and in developing written literary expression in a Philippine language.

A multidisciplinary mind

Art, science, education, and community projects

Paintings, sketches, and sculpture

Rizal made drawings, paintings, and sculptural works, including figures modeled in clay and carved in wood. His visual art reflects the same careful observation and curiosity found in his writing. Individual objects are preserved or documented in museums and historical collections.

Overview of art and creative work · Museo ni Rizal / Intramuros Administration

Relief map of Mindanao

During his exile in Dapitan, Rizal helped create a relief map of Mindanao in the town plaza. The project combined geography, practical design, education, and public service, illustrating how his work extended beyond writing.

Overview and historical context

Teaching in Talisay, Dapitan

Rizal established a small school for boys during his exile. Its practical approach included academic subjects, physical activity, and useful skills. Although teaching is not a literary work, it belongs to the wider body of work through which Rizal put his educational ideas into practice.

Museo ni Rizal / Intramuros Administration

Medical and scientific observations

Rizal trained and worked as an ophthalmologist and pursued scientific interests alongside his literary life. His professional medical work and observations are part of his broader intellectual contribution, though they should be distinguished from his novels, poems, and essays.

Selected chronology

How Rizal’s works developed

  • 1879 — “To the Filipino Youth” Rizal’s award-winning poem expresses confidence in the potential of Filipino youth.
  • 1880 — Student drama El Consejo de los Dioses and Junto al Pasig show his early work in theatre and allegory.
  • 1882 — “Love of Country” His essay writing begins to reach readers through the reformist press.
  • 1887 — Noli Me Tángere The first novel presents a searching critique of colonial society.
  • 1889–1890 — Essays and historical research Rizal writes “The Philippines a Century Hence,” “The Indolence of the Filipinos,” and his annotations to Morga’s history.
  • 1891 — El Filibusterismo The sequel explores the consequences of oppression and the dangers of revenge.
  • 1892–1896 — Dapitan and final writings Rizal continues educational, artistic, and community work during exile and writes Mi Último Adiós before his execution.

Why his works remain important

Literature, reform, and national consciousness

Rizal’s works matter not only because of their literary quality but because they challenged readers to examine power, inequality, education, history, and responsibility to the community. His novels and essays helped circulate reformist ideas, while his poems and letters reveal the personal hopes and costs behind that public work. His historical research encouraged Filipinos to look critically at their past, and his translations and teaching reflected a belief that knowledge should reach beyond a small educated circle.

The Philippine government formally recognized the importance of studying Rizal’s life and writings through Republic Act No. 1425, commonly called the Rizal Law, which specifically names Noli Me Tángere and El Filibusterismo.
